# Renovation Planner — Problem Definition

## 1. Context

Renovating a house is not a single project.

For a private homeowner, it is a collection of interconnected projects that may span months or years:

- individual rooms
    
- bathrooms and kitchens
    
- floors, walls and ceilings
    
- electrical and plumbing work
    
- heating and energy improvements
    
- exterior walls and roofs
    
- garden areas
    
- terraces
    
- sheds and auxiliary buildings
    
- landscaping
    
- repairs and maintenance
    

A homeowner may want to improve these areas piece by piece rather than renovate the entire property at once.

The problem is that a first-time renovator usually starts with **a property and an ambition, but without a plan**.

They often do not know:

- what work actually needs to be done
    
- what should be done first
    
- which work depends on other work
    
- what can be done themselves
    
- what requires a professional trade
    
- which materials will be required
    
- what realistic costs are
    
- what budget should be reserved
    
- how long individual activities will take
    
- how contractors should be coordinated
    
- which quotes are reasonable
    
- which decisions have already been made
    
- which documents need to be retained
    
- how the renovation should be broken into manageable projects
    

The homeowner therefore has to become the **planner, purchaser, budget controller, document manager and project manager of their own renovation**, despite usually having little or no experience doing so.

---

# 2. Primary User

The primary user is a private homeowner who wants to renovate or improve their own property.

They are not a professional construction project manager.

They may have:

- little or no renovation experience
    
- limited knowledge of construction methods
    
- limited knowledge of materials
    
- limited knowledge of trades
    
- no reliable understanding of renovation costs
    
- limited experience requesting or evaluating quotes
    
- a finite household budget
    
- limited time available outside work and family life
    

They may perform some work themselves while outsourcing other work to contractors.

Their renovation is likely to happen incrementally:

> House → Area → Room → Project → Work Package → Task

rather than as one professionally planned construction project.

---

# 3. Core Problem

A private renovator needs to transform an initially vague renovation ambition into a **realistic, understandable and manageable renovation plan**, but lacks the knowledge, structure and tooling required to do this confidently.

Information quickly becomes fragmented across:

- notes
    
- photos
    
- spreadsheets
    
- emails
    
- messenger conversations
    
- contractor quotes
    
- invoices
    
- receipts
    
- product links
    
- sketches
    
- floor plans
    
- PDFs
    
- paper documents
    
- browser bookmarks
    
- memories and conversations
    

There is no coherent model connecting:

**the property → the desired changes → required work → materials → contractors → costs → decisions → documents → schedule → actual outcome.**

As the renovation grows, the homeowner loses overview and becomes increasingly dependent on memory, spreadsheets and disconnected documents.

---

# 4. Problem Area: Where Do I Start?

A homeowner may know that they want to renovate a bathroom, terrace or garden, but not what this actually entails.

A goal such as:

> “Renovate the bathroom”

immediately creates dozens of questions.

Does the plumbing need to change?

Does electrical work need to happen first?

Should the floor be removed?

Is waterproofing required?

What needs to be measured?

Which materials need to be selected?

Which trades are involved?

Which work can happen simultaneously?

Which work must happen sequentially?

What can I realistically do myself?

The first problem is therefore not task management.

It is **turning an idea into a plan**.

The homeowner needs help progressively decomposing an ambition:

> Property  
> → Area  
> → Renovation Project  
> → Work Package  
> → Trade / DIY Work  
> → Task  
> → Material / Resource

The system should make a complex renovation understandable without requiring the homeowner to understand construction project management beforehand.

---

# 5. Problem Area: I Don't Know What I Don't Know

First-time renovators suffer from a fundamental knowledge gap.

They cannot easily judge whether their plan is complete because they do not know which activities they may have forgotten.

For example, painting a room may initially appear to require:

- paint
    
- brushes
    
- time
    

But the actual work may involve:

- moving furniture
    
- protecting floors
    
- removing fixtures
    
- filling holes
    
- repairing cracks
    
- sanding
    
- cleaning
    
- priming
    
- masking
    
- painting multiple coats
    
- drying time
    
- reinstalling fixtures
    
- cleanup
    
- waste disposal
    

Missing work creates unexpected:

- cost
    
- purchases
    
- delays
    
- contractor dependencies
    
- rework
    

The homeowner therefore needs more than an empty project-management tool.

They need **guidance that helps them discover the work involved**.

---

# 6. Problem Area: Budget Uncertainty

One of the largest sources of uncertainty is money.

At the beginning of a renovation, the homeowner often has no reliable understanding of:

- likely project cost
    
- labour cost
    
- material cost
    
- equipment cost
    
- delivery cost
    
- disposal cost
    
- permits or professional services
    
- contingency requirements
    

They may have a household budget such as:

> “I can spend €20,000 over the next two years.”

But cannot answer:

> “What can I realistically renovate with that money?”

Estimates also evolve continuously.

A project may move through several cost states:

> Rough Estimate  
> → Budget  
> → Contractor Quote  
> → Purchase / Commitment  
> → Invoice  
> → Payment  
> → Actual Cost

Without connecting these states, homeowners struggle to understand:

- how much they expect to spend
    
- how much they have committed
    
- how much they have already spent
    
- how much remains available
    
- where overruns occurred
    
- whether another project can still be afforded
    

---

# 7. Problem Area: Renovation Prioritisation

A property may contain dozens or hundreds of possible improvements.

Not all of them can or should happen immediately.

The homeowner needs to make trade-offs between:

- urgency
    
- safety
    
- structural necessity
    
- comfort
    
- aesthetics
    
- energy efficiency
    
- cost
    
- available budget
    
- available time
    
- dependencies
    
- seasonal constraints
    
- value added to the property
    

For example:

> Repairing a leaking roof may need to happen before renovating an upstairs room.

Or:

> Running new electrical wiring may need to happen before plastering and painting.

The homeowner therefore needs to answer:

> **What should I renovate next, and why?**

---

# 8. Problem Area: Dependencies and Sequencing

Renovation tasks are rarely independent.

Many activities can only start after another activity has finished.

Examples include:

> demolition → plumbing → electrical → walls → flooring → painting → fixtures

or

> excavation → drainage → foundation → terrace construction → landscaping

A wrong sequence can create:

- unnecessary rework
    
- damaged finished surfaces
    
- contractor delays
    
- additional cost
    
- duplicated work
    

A first-time renovator may not recognise these dependencies until the mistake has already happened.

The problem is therefore not merely creating a to-do list.

It is understanding the **logical construction sequence**.

---

# 9. Problem Area: Materials and Procurement

Renovation projects require large numbers of materials and products.

The homeowner has to determine:

- what is needed
    
- how much is needed
    
- which product is suitable
    
- which alternatives exist
    
- where it can be purchased
    
- what it costs
    
- whether it is already owned
    
- whether it has already been ordered
    
- when it will arrive
    
- where it will be used
    

The same item may move through several states:

> Idea  
> → Candidate Product  
> → Compared  
> → Selected  
> → Planned  
> → Ordered  
> → Delivered  
> → Used

Without this structure, homeowners risk:

- buying the wrong quantity
    
- purchasing incompatible products
    
- forgetting necessary items
    
- buying the same item twice
    
- delaying contractors because material has not arrived
    
- losing track of product information
    

---

# 10. Problem Area: Price Comparison

Homeowners frequently make decisions between alternative:

- products
    
- materials
    
- suppliers
    
- contractors
    
- construction approaches
    

These decisions involve more than the cheapest price.

A useful comparison may involve:

- purchase price
    
- delivery
    
- quantity
    
- unit price
    
- quality
    
- specifications
    
- warranty
    
- availability
    
- lead time
    
- installation requirements
    
- contractor recommendation
    

Today these comparisons are often scattered between browser tabs, screenshots, notes and spreadsheets.

The homeowner needs a way to preserve **what was compared and why a decision was made**.

---

# 11. Problem Area: Contractor Management

Many renovation activities require professional trades.

The homeowner must effectively perform lightweight procurement.

They need to:

- identify suitable contractors
    
- contact them
    
- explain the required work
    
- arrange site visits
    
- request quotes
    
- compare offers
    
- clarify questions
    
- select contractors
    
- schedule work
    
- track commitments
    
- document changes
    
- record invoices and payments
    

One renovation project may involve several contractors:

- electrician
    
- plumber
    
- carpenter
    
- roofer
    
- tiler
    
- painter
    
- landscaper
    
- architect
    
- structural engineer
    

Each contractor introduces additional communication, documentation and scheduling dependencies.

---

# 12. Problem Area: Quote Management

Contractor quotes are difficult to compare.

They may differ in:

- scope
    
- materials
    
- labour
    
- assumptions
    
- exclusions
    
- quantities
    
- quality
    
- schedule
    
- warranty
    
- payment terms
    

A €5,000 offer and a €7,000 offer may therefore describe substantially different work.

The homeowner needs to understand:

> What exactly am I buying?

and:

> Are these two quotes actually comparable?

Quotes also represent important historical information once a contractor has been selected.

They should remain connected to:

- the project
    
- affected areas
    
- planned work
    
- contractor
    
- budget
    
- invoices
    
- eventual actual costs
    

---

# 13. Problem Area: Document Chaos

A renovation produces a large amount of information.

Examples include:

- photos
    
- measurements
    
- sketches
    
- floor plans
    
- product manuals
    
- data sheets
    
- contractor quotes
    
- invoices
    
- receipts
    
- warranties
    
- contracts
    
- permits
    
- correspondence
    
- inspection reports
    
- before/after documentation
    

These documents often arrive through many channels and become difficult to retrieve months or years later.

The homeowner needs to answer questions such as:

> Which tiles did we install in the bathroom?

> Which paint colour did we use?

> Who installed the heating system?

> Where is the warranty?

> How much did the terrace ultimately cost?

The renovation planner should therefore gradually become the **long-term record of the property**, not merely a temporary project plan.

---

# 14. Problem Area: Capturing Reality During the Renovation

Renovation does not happen primarily at a desk.

The homeowner is frequently:

- standing inside a room
    
- walking through the garden
    
- visiting a hardware store
    
- talking to a contractor
    
- inspecting ongoing work
    

Important information appears in those moments.

The homeowner may need to quickly:

- take a photo
    
- record a measurement
    
- write a note
    
- capture a problem
    
- create a task
    
- record an expense
    
- save a product
    
- document completed work
    

If capturing that information requires significant effort, it will often never be documented.

---

# 15. Problem Area: Plans Need to Be Communicated

Renovation plans do not exist solely for the homeowner.

They need to be communicated to:

- partners
    
- family members
    
- contractors
    
- architects
    
- tradespeople
    
- suppliers
    
- friends helping with DIY work
    

A written task such as:

> “Build terrace”

is insufficient.

People need context:

- Where?
    
- What dimensions?
    
- What should it look like?
    
- Which materials?
    
- Which reference images?
    
- Which work has already been completed?
    
- Which constraints exist?
    
- What exactly should be quoted?
    

The homeowner therefore needs a way to create a **shared understanding of the intended result**.

---

# 16. Problem Area: Planned vs. Actual

Renovations rarely happen exactly as planned.

During execution:

- prices change
    
- damage is discovered
    
- plans change
    
- contractors become unavailable
    
- products become unavailable
    
- tasks take longer than expected
    
- additional work becomes necessary
    

The homeowner therefore needs both:

**Plan**

and

**Reality**

The system should preserve this difference instead of overwriting the original assumptions.

This allows the homeowner to understand:

> What did I expect?

> What actually happened?

> Why did the difference occur?

---

# 17. Emotional Dimension

The practical problems produce an important emotional consequence.

Renovation can feel overwhelming.

The homeowner is constantly confronted with decisions about things they do not fully understand while spending significant amounts of their own money.

This can create:

- uncertainty
    
- fear of making expensive mistakes
    
- decision fatigue
    
- loss of overview
    
- hesitation to start
    
- conflict between household members
    
- distrust of contractor estimates
    

The desired feeling is not:

> “I have project-management software.”

It is:

> **“I understand my renovation and know what to do next.”**

---

# 18. Core User Need

The homeowner needs a system that helps them progressively transform:

> **I want to renovate my property**

into:

> **I understand what needs to be done, in what order, by whom, with which materials, at approximately what cost, and what I should do next.**

---

# 19. Primary Problem Statement

> Private homeowners undertaking renovations often have little experience planning construction work, estimating costs, selecting materials, coordinating contractors or managing dependencies.
> 
> They start with a desired outcome — such as renovating a room, terrace or garden — but lack the structure and knowledge necessary to transform that goal into a realistic plan.
> 
> As the renovation progresses, plans, tasks, costs, quotes, purchases, documents, photos and decisions become fragmented across different tools and communication channels. This makes it difficult to understand the overall project, prioritise work, control the budget and confidently decide what to do next.
> 
> Renovation Planner should help an inexperienced homeowner progressively turn their property and renovation ideas into understandable, affordable and manageable renovation projects while maintaining a coherent record of the decisions, work, people, materials, costs and documents involved.

---

# 20. Jobs to Be Done

## Primary Job

> **When I want to improve my property but don't know how to organise the renovation, help me turn my ideas into a realistic sequence of manageable projects so that I understand what I need to do next and can renovate without losing control of my money, time and decisions.**

## Supporting Jobs

### Understand

> When I consider renovating part of my property, help me understand what work may be involved so that I don't overlook important activities.

### Break Down

> When a renovation feels too large, help me divide it into manageable projects and tasks so that I can make progress piece by piece.

### Prioritise

> When I have many things I want to renovate, help me understand what should happen first so that I spend my time and money wisely.

### Estimate

> When planning work, help me understand the likely cost so that I can decide whether I can afford it.

### Budget

> When several renovation projects compete for my available money, help me allocate and track my budget so that I don't accidentally overcommit.

### Sequence

> When several activities depend on each other, help me determine the correct order so that I avoid unnecessary rework and delays.

### Procure

> When I need products or materials, help me identify, compare, select and track them so that the right things are available when needed.

### Hire

> When professional work is required, help me organise contractors and their work so that responsibilities and commitments remain clear.

### Compare Quotes

> When contractors provide different offers, help me compare their scope and cost so that I can make an informed decision.

### Document

> When renovation information accumulates, help me keep photos, plans, receipts, quotes, manuals and decisions connected to the relevant part of my property.

### Capture

> When I discover something while walking around my property or talking to a contractor, let me capture it immediately so that important information is not lost.

### Communicate

> When I need someone else to understand my renovation idea, help me communicate the planned result and relevant context clearly.

### Track

> When renovation work begins, help me understand what has been planned, committed, purchased, completed and paid.

### Remember

> When I need information about the property months or years later, help me retrieve what was installed, changed, purchased and paid for.

---

# 21. Product Principle

The Renovation Planner should not assume that the user already knows how to manage a renovation.

Therefore:

> **The product should teach structure through use rather than requiring project-management knowledge before use.**

An empty project management system would force the homeowner to already understand the problem.

Renovation Planner should instead progressively guide the homeowner from:

**Property**

to:

**Idea → Project → Scope → Work → Materials → People → Cost → Schedule → Execution → Property History**

---
