# Product discovery blueprint

Use this blueprint to move from an audience to an evidence-backed product opportunity, a
testable business advantage, and a focused prototype. Complete the sections in order. Treat
assumptions as hypotheses, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and keep links to every
source.

## 1. The customer

Start with the customer rather than a chosen product or feature.

### Assignment

Find a worthwhile opportunity for the defined audience. Do not assume the business category,
problem, or solution unless the brief already fixes one.

1. Identify recurring customer problems.
2. Group related problems into opportunity areas.
3. Compare the opportunities.
4. Select one opportunity and explain why it deserves further investigation.

## 2. The evidence

Ground the shortlist in real customer language and existing behavior.

### Assignment

Research genuine customer conversations and examine at least three existing alternatives. Use
methods and sources you can access legitimately.

Look for:

- repeated frustrations;
- current workarounds;
- switching signals;
- spending or purchasing signals;
- reasons customers stay with an imperfect alternative;
- counterevidence that weakens the opportunity.

Collect at least six distinct, relevant conversations across more than one source when possible.
Link to the original material. State access limitations and evidence gaps. Never invent a source
to satisfy a target, and do not treat popularity by itself as proof of willingness to pay.

## 3. The business

Assume customers and competitors can reproduce competent software quickly. A feature list is not
a durable advantage.

### Assignment

Explain:

1. what a capable competitor could copy in a weekend;
2. what valuable advantage would still be missing;
3. why a customer would pay instead of building, combining tools, or switching;
4. how the business could earn that advantage from zero;
5. how the first customer would be acquired;
6. which experiment could disprove the advantage.

The product must create value before the proposed advantage exists. Treat defensibility as a
hypothesis, not an established moat.

## 4. The plan

Turn the research into one connected, inspectable product plan.

### Assignment

Create an editable visual canvas with distinct but connected areas for:

1. customer evidence;
2. alternatives and opportunity;
3. the main user journey;
4. product screens and visual direction;
5. architecture and data flow;
6. MVP scope and build sequence.

Use a purposeful combination of diagrams, images, concise annotations, and source links. Every
important product decision should trace back to evidence or be labeled as an assumption.

### Plan checks

- [ ] The chosen customer and core problem are unambiguous.
- [ ] Evidence and counterevidence are visible.
- [ ] The journey shows a complete outcome, not isolated features.
- [ ] Screens map to steps in that journey.
- [ ] Architecture supports only the scoped workflow.
- [ ] The MVP sequence delivers customer value early.
- [ ] Sources and assumptions are clearly distinguishable.
- [ ] The canvas remains editable.

## 5. The product

Build a focused proof of the chosen opportunity rather than a broad product shell.

### Assignment

Create a polished prototype and a planning site that present the explorable product plan as one
experience.

Constrain the prototype to:

- one primary user role;
- one core problem;
- one complete workflow;
- three to five meaningful steps;
- no more than three main product screens;
- realistic sample data;
- state that survives a refresh.

Keep research and planning material in supporting views. Exclude production authentication,
payments, live messaging, and external integrations unless one is essential to test the core
hypothesis. Label every simulated behavior clearly.
