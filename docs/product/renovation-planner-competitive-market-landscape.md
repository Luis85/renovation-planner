# Competitive & Market Landscape Analysis
## Renovation Planner for Obsidian

**Status:** Research Draft  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 2026-08-24

# 1. Executive Summary

Renovation Planner enters a fragmented but substantial market. Strong products exist for floor plans and visualization, property capture and estimating, professional construction management, inspiration/contractor discovery, and generic project management. Far fewer products treat the **homeowner's complete renovation as a durable knowledge-and-planning system** spanning:

```text
existing property → desired change → spaces → work → quantities → budget
→ materials → trades → schedule → execution → decisions → documents
→ actual result → long-term property knowledge
```

The strongest strategic position is therefore not "another floor planner" and not "Buildertrend for consumers."

> **Renovation Planner can become a local-first renovation operating system for homeowners and serious DIY renovators that connects the physical property to work, cost, decisions, documentation and execution.**

Obsidian adds a meaningful differentiator: transparent, user-owned Markdown knowledge instead of a specialized SaaS silo.

# 2. Market Context

## 2.1 Large, persistent category

Harvard JCHS projects U.S. homeowner improvement and maintenance spending at about **$518B by the end of 2026**, despite slowing growth. Houzz's 2026 U.S. study reports 54% of surveyed homeowners renovated in 2025, median spend was $20,000, and the 90th percentile reached $150,000. Half planned renovations for 2026.

## 2.2 Planning is a major part of the job

Houzz reports planning often exceeds construction time; kitchens averaged roughly 9.5 months planning and 5.8 months construction in its 2026 U.S. research. This supports a product centered on the long planning journey, not only execution tracking.

## 2.3 Budget failure is common

Houzz reports 75% established a budget and 37% exceeded it. Reported overrun drivers included unexpectedly expensive products/services (52%), upgraded materials (35%), unexpected complexity (32%), scope/design changes (31%) and construction issues (22%).

This validates a progression such as:

```text
rough estimate → detailed estimate → quantities → selections
→ committed cost → actual cost → change impact
```

## 2.4 Renovation is usually hybrid

Houzz reports 91% of U.S. renovators hired at least one professional in 2025; the 2026 UK study reports 92%. The realistic user model is therefore:

```text
homeowner + DIY work + specialist trades + possibly contractor/designer
```

rather than a strict DIY/professional split.

# 3. Market Segmentation

## A — Consumer spatial & interior design

**Examples:** Planner 5D, Floorplanner, RoomSketcher, HomeByMe, Homestyler, Coohom.

**Core job:** Help me design and visualize what my home could look like.

**Strengths:** floor plans, furniture, materials, 3D, rendering, experimentation.

**Typical gap:** work breakdown, trade coordination, change tracking, execution, project history and documentation lifecycle.

## B — Property capture & estimating

**Primary example:** magicplan.

**Core job:** Capture a property accurately and turn measurements into documentation and estimates.

Strong in mobile capture, LiDAR, measurements, floor plans, reporting and estimation. This is one of the closest capability categories.

## C — Professional remodeling platforms

**Examples:** Houzz Pro, Buildertrend, BuildBook, JobTread.

**Core job:** Help my remodeling/construction business sell, plan, deliver and financially manage projects.

Strong in estimates, proposals, schedules, change orders, clients, subcontractors, invoices and job costing. Their weakness as direct competitors is that they are business-first subscription products and generally excessive for homeowners.

## D — Inspiration & marketplace

**Primary example:** Houzz consumer.

Strong in inspiration, products and professional discovery, but not necessarily the homeowner's operational source of truth.

## E — Generic project/knowledge tools

**Examples:** Obsidian, Notion, Trello, Airtable, spreadsheets.

Their advantage is flexibility; their weakness is that the user must invent renovation semantics, spatial relationships, quantities and planning structure.

## F — CAD / professional design

**Examples:** SketchUp and professional CAD/BIM tools.

Extremely capable built-environment modeling, but a different job with a much higher learning curve.

# 4. Direct Competitors

## 4.1 magicplan — Closest capability competitor

Current capabilities include real-time floor-plan creation, manual/laser measurements, LiDAR on supported iOS devices, reports, photos, floor-plan-based estimating, custom item libraries and automatic material/labor calculations.

**Strengths**
- excellent mobile property capture
- measurements are first-class
- spatial information feeds estimating
- professional reports
- mature field workflow

**Opportunity against it**
- homeowner-first journey
- richer research/decision knowledge
- project narrative and documentation
- Obsidian-native linking
- transparent Markdown/local-first data
- long-lived property knowledge

**Threat:** **High.** If Renovation Planner becomes merely "floor plan + quantities + estimate," magicplan already has a mature proposition.

## 4.2 Planner 5D

Planner 5D combines consumer 2D/3D design with a large catalog, AI floor-plan recognition, rendering and a Budget Widget; its Professional tier adds CAD export, Specs & Docs Organizer and Price Estimator.

**Strengths:** accessibility, visualization, consumer reach, catalog, AI design.

**Gap:** center of gravity remains `design → furnish → visualize → present`, not full renovation delivery.

**Threat:** **High for Design; Medium for renovation management.**

## 4.3 Floorplanner

Mature 2D/3D editor with free casual use, a huge object/material library, project upgrades and professional options. Floorplanner reports more than 30M registered users.

**Strengths:** mature editor, low entry barrier, 2D/3D, huge catalog.

**Gap:** models space/design rather than renovation execution.

**Threat:** **High for floor-plan creation; Low-Medium overall.**

## 4.4 RoomSketcher

Provides 2D/3D plans, measurements, total area, material replacement, Live 3D, 360 views, blueprint workflows and floor capture/AI conversion.

**Strengths:** polished plans, measurements, accessible professional output.

**Gap:** spatial/design project rather than renovation operating model.

**Threat:** **Medium.**

## 4.5 HomeByMe

Offers 2D/3D projects, rendering, a large branded product catalog, furniture creation, product/shopping connection and plan export.

**Strengths:** visualization, branded products, consumer friendliness.

**Gap:** journey ends closer to `design → visualize → select` than `plan → execute → track actual outcome`.

**Threat:** **Medium.**

## 4.6 Houzz / Houzz Pro

Consumer Houzz is a powerful inspiration, product and professional-discovery ecosystem.

Houzz Pro covers 3D floor plans, takeoffs, estimates, proposals, product libraries, schedules, tasks, change orders, invoices, payments, job costing, CRM and client collaboration. It can turn takeoffs into estimates and estimates into schedules.

**Strategic distinction:**

```text
Houzz Pro:
How do I run my remodeling business and client projects?

Renovation Planner:
How do I understand, plan and manage the renovation of my property?
```

**Threat:** **Very High if Renovation Planner drifts into contractor software; Medium with strong homeowner/local-first positioning.**

## 4.7 BuildBook

Residential construction software for builders/remodelers, deliberately positioned as easier to adopt than heavier suites. Current published pricing starts around $79/month annually for a solo account.

**Threat:** **Low-Medium direct; useful UX benchmark.**

## 4.8 Buildertrend

Broad cloud construction-management suite for builders, remodelers and specialty contractors with custom pricing.

**Threat:** **Low direct; High capability benchmark.**

## 4.9 JobTread

Construction management and estimating platform. Current published pricing is $199/month plus internal users; external vendor/subcontractor/customer users can access a limited portal.

**Threat:** **Low direct; Medium capability benchmark.**

# 5. The Most Important Competitor: The Improvised Stack

The strongest practical competitor may be:

```text
Excel / Google Sheets
+ notes
+ paper
+ WhatsApp
+ photos
+ browser bookmarks
+ calendar
+ contractor PDFs
+ memory
```

This stack is familiar, flexible, often free and easy to adopt incrementally. Its weakness is fragmentation.

The key product question is therefore not "Are we better than Excel?" but:

> **Does connecting space, work, cost, materials, decisions and documents create enough value to justify a dedicated renovation workspace?**

This should remain a core user-research question.

# 6. Obsidian-Native Landscape

Recent Obsidian community activity shows demand for project landing pages, Kanban, Gantt, tables, dependencies, custom statuses and Markdown/YAML-backed tasks.

Community discussions repeatedly value:

- plain files,
- avoiding proprietary lock-in,
- links between tasks and notes,
- plugin removal without data loss,
- integration with the rest of the vault.

Generic Obsidian project-management plugins overlap with Renovation Planner in:

```text
tasks + schedule + dashboard + statuses + dependencies
```

Renovation Planner must differentiate through:

```text
property + spatial context + renovation semantics + quantities
+ cost + materials + work + documentation
```

Generic task management should be supporting infrastructure, not the product's identity.

# 8. Strategic White Space

## 8.1 The missing bridge between visualization and execution

Consumer products are excellent at:

```text
What could my room look like?
```

Professional platforms are excellent at:

```text
How do I commercially deliver this job?
```

The underserved middle is:

```text
What exactly am I changing?
What work does that create?
What will it cost?
What do I need?
Who does what?
What happens next?
What changed?
Where is the evidence?
What did we actually build?
```

This is the primary white space.

## 8.2 Property as durable knowledge

Most competing tools treat a project as something that ends.

Obsidian enables a different model:

```text
Property
├── existing state
├── renovation projects
├── products installed
├── manuals
├── warranties
├── costs
├── decisions
├── photos
├── maintenance
└── future renovation
```

The renovation can become part of a **long-lived digital property record**.

This could become a major long-term differentiator.

## 8.3 Planning without mandatory 3D

Many consumer products pull users rapidly toward visual design. Renovation Planner can support:

```text
rooms + work + costs + documents
```

before requiring a floor plan.

That lowers onboarding cost and broadens the product beyond aesthetic redesign.

## 8.4 Rough-to-precise progression

Professional tools tend to assume structured estimates; design tools often have little execution costing.

Renovation Planner can deliberately support:

```text
€2,500 rough estimate
        ↓
24.3 m² floor
        ↓
26.7 m² including waste
        ↓
tiles + adhesive + grout + labor
        ↓
supplier quotation
        ↓
committed cost
        ↓
invoice / actual cost
```

This maps well to how uncertainty decreases over a real renovation.

## 8.5 Decisions as first-class project data

A large part of renovation work is deciding:

```text
Which layout?
Which material?
Which contractor?
Repair or replace?
Do now or later?
DIY or outsource?
Accept this scope change?
```

Most competitors focus more strongly on outputs than on the decision history that produced them.

Obsidian is naturally suited to this.

# 10. Proposed Positioning

## Category

**Local-first renovation planning workspace**

or, more ambitiously:

**Renovation operating system for your home**

## Core proposition

> Plan your renovation from the property outward: connect rooms and plans with work, materials, costs, trades, decisions, schedules and documentation while keeping the entire project in your Obsidian Vault.

## Differentiators

1. **Project-first, not editor-first**
2. **Space × work × cost integration**
3. **Progressive complexity**
4. **No mandatory floor plan**
5. **Local-first and user-owned**
6. **Markdown-native knowledge**
7. **Decisions and documentation beside structured project data**
8. **Useful from first idea through completed/as-built state**
9. **DIY + professional hybrid workflow**
10. **Long-term property knowledge**

# 11. What Not to Compete On

## Do not lead with photorealistic rendering

Planner 5D, HomeByMe, Floorplanner, RoomSketcher and Houzz have large investments in rendering and asset catalogs.

Integrate or remain "good enough" spatially instead of making render quality the product thesis.

## Do not become contractor ERP

Avoid early expansion into:

- CRM,
- sales pipeline,
- payroll,
- accounting,
- customer invoicing,
- subcontractor portals,
- business profitability.

Those pull the product directly into mature professional platforms.

## Do not become generic Obsidian project management

Tasks, Gantt and Kanban are useful, but generic Obsidian plugins already address them.

Every project-management feature should answer:

> What is specifically different because this is a renovation?

# 12. Product Opportunities Derived from the Landscape

## Opportunity 1 — Renovation Project Home

A persistent orientation layer answering:

```text
Where are we?
What is incomplete?
What is over budget?
What happens next?
```

## Opportunity 2 — Spatial Context as Project Context

Selecting Kitchen should scope:

```text
plan + work + materials + costs + documents + photos + decisions
```

This is more valuable than a standalone floor-plan editor.

## Opportunity 3 — Renovation Guidance Engine

Deterministic guidance can identify:

- uncalibrated plans,
- spaces without work,
- work without estimates,
- unresolved decisions,
- unscheduled work,
- missing materials,
- cost variance,
- blockers.

This creates an "assistant" without requiring AI.

## Opportunity 4 — Decision Register

Capture decision, alternatives, rationale, cost/schedule impact and related room/work.

## Opportunity 5 — Change Control for Homeowners

A lightweight change model:

```text
Proposed change
→ affected scope
→ cost delta
→ schedule delta
→ decision
→ updated baseline
```

Professional platforms do this commercially; homeowner tools rarely make it central.

## Opportunity 6 — Property Knowledge Base

After renovation, preserve:

- as-built plans,
- installed products,
- paint/material references,
- manuals,
- warranties,
- invoices,
- contractor details,
- maintenance information.

This extends value beyond project completion.

## Opportunity 7 — Contractor Brief / Scope Export

Generate a clean scope package from homeowner-owned data:

```text
room
+ desired outcome
+ work
+ measurements
+ photos
+ materials
+ constraints
```

This could improve quotation quality without turning Renovation Planner into a contractor marketplace.

## Opportunity 8 — Progressive Estimation

Keep rough estimates useful while allowing deeper takeoff/quantity refinement later.

# 13. Key Risks

## Risk — Spatial editor becomes the product

If most development effort goes into drawing/rendering, the product enters a crowded market with mature competitors.

**Mitigation:** treat spatial editing as one capability inside the renovation journey.

## Risk — Feature explosion

Professional construction suites demonstrate how large this domain can become.

**Mitigation:** use JTBD evidence to decide what belongs in the homeowner product.

## Risk — Homeowner/professional identity confusion

Trying to satisfy contractors equally may compromise simplicity.

**Mitigation:** homeowner is primary actor; professionals are participants/resources.

## Risk — Obsidian-only market ceiling

Obsidian creates differentiation but limits addressable distribution.

**Mitigation:** architect domain/application layers independently enough that a future standalone shell remains possible without weakening the current plugin thesis.

## Risk — Data model overwhelms users

Construction terminology can rapidly introduce zones, assemblies, cost codes, work packages, dependencies and procurement states.

**Mitigation:** progressive disclosure and plain-language defaults.

# 14. Market Hypotheses to Validate

1. Serious DIY renovators experience enough fragmentation to adopt a dedicated workspace.
2. Users value room/space context beyond ordinary project folders.
3. A no-floor-plan workflow materially improves onboarding.
4. Users want rough estimates before detailed takeoffs.
5. Homeowners actively need contractor/trade coordination but not contractor-business software.
6. Change impact is painful enough to deserve explicit tooling.
7. Renovation decisions are currently poorly documented.
8. Long-term property documentation creates value after project completion.
9. Local-first/open files are meaningful differentiators to the Obsidian audience.
10. Users prefer one coherent renovation model over assembling multiple Obsidian plugins.
11. A visual editor is important but not the main retention driver.
12. Contractor-ready scope export could be more valuable than contractor marketplace functionality.

# 19. Strategic Thesis

The market does not lack floor planners.

It does not lack task managers.

It does not lack contractor software.

What appears less well served is the homeowner who needs to connect all of these questions:

```text
What exists?
What do I want to change?
Where?
Why?
What work does that require?
What does it depend on?
Who will do it?
What do I need to buy?
What will it cost?
When should it happen?
What changed?
What is blocked?
What did I decide?
Where is the documentation?
What did we actually build?
```

Renovation Planner should own that connected problem.

The long-term moat is unlikely to be the canvas itself.

The stronger moat is the **structured renovation knowledge graph inside a user-owned vault**, where physical spaces, work, products, costs, people, decisions and documents remain connected throughout the life of the property.
